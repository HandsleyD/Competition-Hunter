# Competition Hunter — design options

Status: **proposal, pending decisions** (see [Open decisions](#9-open-decisions)).
Purpose: give the implementation session a spec to build from.

---

## 1. Goal and shape of the problem

Find UK free-to-enter competitions, filter them down to the ones actually worth
a human's time, and make entering them as close to one click as possible.

"Comping" is a well-established UK hobby with a known economics: the winners are
the people who enter *consistently*, *early*, and who preferentially enter comps
with **fewer entrants** rather than bigger prizes. Most of the value of software
here is in the **finding, filtering, deduping and tracking** layers, not in
hammering submit buttons. That is worth stating up front because it changes what
we build.

Three distinct problems, with very different difficulty and risk:

| Layer | Difficulty | Risk | Value |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Discovery** — find comps across sources | Low–medium | Low | High |
| **Triage** — dedupe, rank, track, remind | Medium | None | **Highest** |
| **Entry** — actually fill the forms | High | Medium–high | Medium |

The first two are where the leverage is. The third is where every previous
attempt at this gets stuck.

---

## 2. The constraint that shapes everything: automated entry is prohibited

Effectively every UK promotion's T&Cs bans automated entry. This is not a
fringe clause — it is boilerplate:

- Lidl: *"syndicated entries or those made using methods such as a computer
  macro, a script or the use of automated devices or processes are not allowed,
  and all such entries will be disqualified"*
- Audi: *"entries generated by script, macro, mechanical or other automated
  means are prohibited and will cause disqualification"*
- Omaze: *"use, or attempted use, of any robotic, automated, programmed,
  mechanical, or other non-manual entry methods, will void all entries"*

Practical consequences:

- A bot that auto-submits **voids the wins it generates**. You do not get
  disqualified at entry time; you get disqualified at prize-claim time, which is
  the worst possible moment — after you've already "won".
- Social comps (X/Instagram follow-RT-comment) additionally breach the
  *platform's* automation rules, which risks the account itself.
- CAPTCHAs are a deliberate "no bots" signal. **This project will not solve,
  bypass or outsource CAPTCHAs.** Where one appears, the human handles it.

So the design below treats **human-in-the-loop entry as the target**, not as a
fallback. The realistic end state is: *the machine finds it, ranks it, opens it,
and fills it; you look at it and press submit.* Entry time per comp drops from
~90 seconds to ~5, which is the actual win — 20x throughput without voiding
anything.

Tier 3 (full unattended submit) is documented in §7 for completeness, and the
architecture doesn't forbid it, but it is not recommended and should not be the
default.

## 3. Sourcing options

### Option A — Official RSS feeds ✅ recommended as the spine

**ThePrizeFinder publishes RSS feeds** at `/feeds`: *New Competitions*,
*Top Prizes* (>£100), and *Closing Soon* (next 3 days). These exist explicitly
to be consumed by software.

- Structured, stable, polite, zero ToS ambiguity, trivially parsed.
- `Closing Soon` and `New` together give you both ends of the funnel.
- Other aggregators (Loquax, Competition Database, Latest Deals) should be
  checked for equivalents during implementation.

**Verdict: start here.** It's the highest quality-per-unit-effort source.

### Option B — Newsletter inbox parsing ✅ recommended alongside A

Create a dedicated comping Gmail account, subscribe it to every aggregator's
newsletter (ThePrizeFinder, Loquax, Competitions Time all have them), and parse
the inbound mail.

This is the genuinely underrated route:

- You are the intended recipient. No scraping, no robots.txt question, no
  rate-limit, no bot-detection, nothing to break when a site redesigns.
- Newsletters are *curated* — an editor already did a quality pass.
- The same inbox is where confirmations and **win notifications** land, which
  closes the loop (§6).
- A dedicated address is standard comping practice anyway, because entering
  comps generates an enormous volume of marketing email you do not want in your
  real inbox.

### Option C — HTML scraping of listing pages

Per-site adapters against e.g. Competitions Time, Loquax, Competition Database.

- Pro: best coverage; some good sites have no feed.
- Con: brittle, needs per-site maintenance, and needs a robots.txt/ToS check per
  site before we touch it. Rate-limit hard (1 req / few seconds, cached,
  conditional GETs, honest User-Agent).
- Sitemaps (`/sitemap.xml`) are a politer middle path where offered.

**Verdict: add selectively in phase 2**, one adapter at a time, only for sites
that clearly permit it and that add coverage the feeds miss.

### Option D — Direct / primary sources

Brand pages, ITV & radio comps, magazine dailies, Gleam's public giveaway
directory, X/Instagram hashtag search.

- Pro: you find comps *before* the aggregators list them, which is where the
  best odds are — a comp that hasn't hit the aggregators yet has a fraction of
  the entrants.
- Con: very noisy; social APIs are now paid and heavily rate-limited.

**Verdict: phase 3, and the most interesting long-term edge.**

### Option E — Aggregating the aggregators

Not a separate source so much as the point: run A+B+C together and treat
**the number of sources listing a comp as a negative ranking signal** (§5).
Widely-listed comps have the most entrants.

## 4. Architecture

```
  RSS feeds ─┐
  newsletters├─► ingest adapters ─► normalise ─► dedupe ─► enrich ─► score ─► surface
  scrapers  ─┘      (per source)     (schema)   (canon.   (LLM)   (EV/min)   │
                                                  URL)                        │
                                                                    ┌─────────┴─────────┐
                                                                    │                   │
                                                              daily digest        entry assist
                                                             (email/dashboard)     (local only)
```

**Split the system across two trust zones** — this matters:

| | Runs where | Holds | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Discovery + triage** | GitHub Actions cron (free, open network) | Only public data | No secrets, no PII, nothing sensitive. Actions gives you scheduled runs with no server to pay for or maintain. |
| **Entry assist** | Your own machine, on demand | Your name, address, DOB, phone, email — the autofill profile | Personal data should never be in the repo or in CI, even a private one. Your browser profile and real IP also make entries look like what they are: yours. |

Two further notes on placement:

- **This Claude Code environment cannot reach the comping sites.** Its egress
  proxy 403s `loquax.co.uk`, `theprizefinder.com`, `competitionstime.co.uk` and
  `moneysavingexpert.com`. So the implementation session can write and unit-test
  the adapters here against recorded fixtures, but cannot do live integration
  runs unless the environment's network policy is widened. GitHub Actions has
  open network access, which is another reason to put ingest there.
- **Storage: SQLite**, committed as a file or held in an Actions cache/artifact.
  A personal-scale project will hold tens of thousands of rows at most; Postgres
  is unnecessary overhead. Use Turso/libSQL only if a hosted dashboard needs
  read access.

## 5. Triage: dedupe and ranking

This is the part that makes the tool worth having.

### Dedupe

The same comp appears on six aggregators, each wrapping the link in its own
affiliate/tracking redirect. So:

1. Follow redirects to the **true destination URL**.
2. Strip tracking params (`utm_*`, `ref`, affiliate ids) to a canonical form.
3. Fuzzy-match on `(promoter, prize, closing date)` for the ones that still slip
   through.
4. Keep a record of *how many sources listed it* — that's a ranking input, not
   noise to discard.

### Ranking — optimise for £ per minute, not prize size

The naive tool sorts by prize value and enters the £10,000 holiday that 400,000
other people also entered. The ranking should approximate:

```
score  =  (prize_value × P(win))  /  entry_effort_minutes
```

`P(win)` isn't observable, but good proxies are:

| Signal | Effect on odds | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Requires a tiebreaker / skill question** | ▲▲ strongly better | Most people can't be bothered. Often cuts entrants by an order of magnitude. |
| Listed on only 1 source, or not yet aggregated | ▲▲ better | Hasn't hit the mass-comping audience yet |
| Regional / niche / trade restriction | ▲ better | Smaller eligible pool |
| Requires receipt, proof of purchase, effort | ▲ better | Filters out casual entrants |
| Long-running, listed everywhere, huge headline prize | ▼▼ worse | Maximum competition |
| Closing very soon | ▼ worse (but urgent) | Already had full exposure |

Flags to extract and filter on: **UK-only**, **18+**, **purchase necessary**,
**skill vs. free draw**, **daily/weekly repeatable**, **closing date**.

An LLM pass is the right tool for this enrichment — reading a free-text
competition description and pulling out prize value, closing date, restrictions
and entry mechanic generalises far better than regex, and it's cheap at this
volume (a few hundred items a day).

### Tracking — the highest-value feature

Many comps are **daily-entry**. Knowing *which ones you've already entered
today* is the single thing every serious comper needs and the thing spreadsheets
do worst. The store should hold entry history per comp per day, and the daily
digest should lead with "your repeatable comps, X of Y done today".

## 6. Surfacing

Options, roughly in order of build cost:

1. **Daily digest email** — ranked shortlist, deep links, "repeatables not yet
   done today" at the top. Lowest effort, works on a phone, fits the daily rhythm
   of comping.
2. **Static dashboard** — generated HTML published to GitHub Pages or as an
   Artifact; sortable, filterable, shows the tracker state.
3. **Telegram / push bot** — best for time-sensitive "closing in 2 hours, high
   score" alerts.

Recommendation: **(1) first**, add (2) once there's data worth browsing.

**Closing the loop:** the same dedicated inbox that receives the newsletters
receives the win notifications. A classifier pass over it gives you a wins
ledger, and — more usefully — a measured hit rate per source and per score band,
which is the only way to know whether the ranking model is actually any good.
Almost nothing else in comping gives you that feedback signal.

## 7. The entry layer

Three tiers. The architecture should let you sit anywhere on this, but the
default should be tier 2.

### Tier 1 — Manual, assisted
Digest gives ranked links; you click through and enter by hand, with browser
autofill or a password manager doing the typing. Zero risk. Already a big
improvement on browsing aggregators yourself.

### Tier 2 — Supervised autofill ✅ recommended
A local Playwright session (or a browser extension) opens the entry page, fills
every field it can from your profile, and **stops**. You glance at it, handle
any CAPTCHA or tickbox, and press submit.

The interesting technical choice here: **don't write per-site selectors.**
Entry forms are wildly heterogeneous, but an LLM given the page's DOM can map
"which field wants my postcode" reliably and generalises to sites it has never
seen. Cache a learned field-map per domain so repeat visits are fast and free,
and fall back to the LLM when the map misses. That's the piece that makes this
better than the decade-old comping tools, which are all brittle selector sets.

Known platform clusters worth special-casing for speed: Gleam.io, ViralSweep,
Rafflecopter, Woobox, ShortStack, plus plain HTML forms.

You remain the one entering. The T&C problem in §2 largely evaporates, because a
human is making each entry decision — which is materially different from a
script submitting 500 entries overnight.

### Tier 3 — Unattended auto-submit ⚠️ not recommended
Technically a small delta from tier 2 (don't stop, just submit). But per §2 it
voids the wins it produces, risks social account bans, and turns a hobby tool
into something that gets you blocklisted by promoters. If you want it anyway
it's your call and a one-line config change — but it should never be the
default, and it should never touch social comps.

## 8. Stack and phasing

**Recommendation: Python.** Best ecosystem for exactly this shape of work
(`feedparser`, `httpx`, `selectolax`, `pydantic`, `playwright`), and the LLM
enrichment and form-mapping pieces are cleanest there. Choose TypeScript instead
only if you want a browser extension as the entry surface, since that would
otherwise split the codebase across two languages.

| Phase | Deliverable | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | RSS ingest → SQLite → dedupe → daily digest email | End-to-end value in the smallest possible build. ThePrizeFinder feeds only. |
| **2** | Newsletter ingest, LLM enrichment, EV scoring, entry tracker | This is where it starts beating doing it by hand. |
| **3** | Supervised autofill (tier 2), local only | The throughput multiplier. |
| **4** | Selective scrapers, dashboard, wins ledger + hit-rate feedback | Coverage and measurement. |

Phase 1 is small — a weekend of Sonnet's time — and worth shipping before
committing to the rest.

## 9. Open decisions

1. **How far up the automation spectrum?** (tier 1 / 2 / 3 — recommend 2)
2. **Where does the scheduled ingest run?** (GitHub Actions / local cron / VPS —
   recommend Actions)
3. **How do you want results delivered?** (digest email / dashboard / Telegram —
   recommend email first)
4. **Language?** (Python / TypeScript — recommend Python unless a browser
   extension is wanted)
5. Do you want a **dedicated comping Gmail + postal address** set up? The
   newsletter source and the wins ledger both depend on it.
